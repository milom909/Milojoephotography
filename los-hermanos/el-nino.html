El Niño winter 2026–27

El Niño is here. Get ready now.

Forecasters expect one of the strongest El Niños on record this winter. Here's what that means for Larkspur, and how a sandbag wall helps.

What it is

Warm ocean, wild winter

El Niño is when the ocean along the equator in the Pacific gets much warmer than normal. That warm water changes the jet stream, which steers storms. During a strong El Niño, more and bigger storms often get aimed at California, and the ocean along our coast sits higher than usual for months.

Why Larkspur

Three kinds of water, all at once

Larkspur sits where Corte Madera Creek meets the bay. That's what makes El Niño so risky here. Each of these is a problem by itself. During El Niño they can all hit together.

Rain

Bigger storms

The heaviest El Niño rain usually comes in January and February. Forecasters say North Bay watersheds like ours are more likely than not to see very wet conditions this winter.

Bay level

A higher bay

Strong El Niños in 1982–83 and 1997–98 raised California sea levels 6 to 10 inches, and it lasted from fall into spring. On Feb. 3, 1998, the high tide at the Golden Gate came in about 2 feet higher than the tide tables predicted.

King tides

Extreme high tides

Low parts of Larkspur and Corte Madera are at risk once tides reach about 7 feet. Last January the tide peaked near 8.7 feet, and the Larkspur Marina flooded. Officials expect 8-foot-plus king tides again this winter.

When the bay is already high, rainwater in Corte Madera Creek has nowhere to go. It backs up over the banks and into the streets, and garages flood first.

We've seen this before

The last super El Niño

The 1997–98 El Niño is the one people around here still talk about. February 1998 brought 14.89 inches of rain to San Francisco. All 10 Bay Area counties, including Marin, were declared eligible for FEMA disaster aid. A Marin emergency official remembered streets in Mill Valley "knee deep in water" and houses sliding down hills.

How sandbagging helps

Hold the line before the water does

Sandbags won't stop a whole creek, and no sandbag wall is perfectly watertight. A wall built the right way does slow water down and turn it away, so a lot less of it gets into your garage or house. Here's what we do and why.

Plastic sheeting first

Flood guides agree that sandbags work best at doorways with a waterproof layer behind them. We lay heavy plastic under the bags and run it up against the door.

Bags turn water away

Officials recommend placing bags so they steer water, mud and debris away from your home instead of trying to dam it. We set every run with that in mind.

Pumps need walls

Larkspur is adding pumps for this winter, but its Public Works Director said pumps only work alongside physical barriers. Your sandbag wall is that barrier at your door.

Up before the storm

Sandbags only help if they're down before the water rises. We set up ahead of big forecasts and king tides, and come back to pick them up when the season's over.

Also sign up for Alert Marin for official flood warnings, and check Ready Marin for county updates.

The honest part

El Niño isn't a guarantee

The Bay Area sits between the Northern and Southern California storm tracks, so the heaviest rain could land north or south of us. The 2015–16 El Niño was very strong, and San Francisco got only about half as much rain as in 1997–98. Still, forecasters say the odds this winter favor wet. Sandbags are cheap compared with a flooded garage, so it makes sense to be ready.
